The AI cuts aren't happening in the building

24 November 2026 · Vikram Mekala

Anthropic put out a report this week called Working with AI. They spoke to people at 58 US companies, 66 interviews in total, about how AI is actually being used at work. Law firms, dentists, accountants, a trucking company, manufacturers, startups. It's worth a read. Here's what stood out for me.

What the report found

  • Most of the value comes from doing more, not cutting costs. People said AI mostly helped them get more done or do better work, rather than save money.
  • Mostly, AI is writing code. And not just for developers. Accountants, ops people and QA supervisors with no coding background are building their own apps. One accountant built an app to check 401(k) payments, a job that used to take 80 hours.
  • Hardly any layoffs. Only two companies had seen AI fully replace a role and someone lose their job.
  • But less hiring. About two thirds of the companies said they're hiring less because of AI. Not firing, just not replacing people or not growing the team.
  • Contractors are getting cut. Nine companies dropped contractors, a lot of them offshore. One finance company let go of more than 100 offshore data and developer contractors.
  • Top-down rollouts struggle. When management picks a workflow and automates it, people worry about their jobs and push back. Some of these tools ended up sitting unused.
  • Bottom-up has limits too. Handing out tools gets you a few power users and some cool side projects, but they don't spread and someone has to maintain them.
  • The bottleneck moves. Once coding gets faster, the slow bits become review, decisions, sales and getting people on board.
  • The best results came from reorganising. Companies that gave people whole workflows to own, moved them onto the work AI can't do yet, and opened up company data to everyone got the most out of it.

The bit I keep thinking about

The contractor finding. Everyone's reading this as "AI isn't costing jobs". It is. They're just on the other side of an outsourcing contract, so they don't show up as layoffs.

The report says why. Outsourced work is broken into small, self-contained tasks you can hand off. That's how outsourcing works. It's also pretty much how you set up an AI agent.

I spent nine years at Satyam, 2000 to 2009, on the Unilever account in Hyderabad, Milan and London. I also did a BP reverse transition, six months handing people, processes and systems over to a new vendor. A lot of that was writing the work down so someone else could pick it up without us. Turns out that's exactly what makes work easy to give to AI.

There's one more thing. The companies doing well with AI are cutting out handoffs, giving one person or a small team the whole job end to end. Outsourcing is basically handoffs. Onshore, offshore, tickets, specs, reviews. That's a tough spot to be in.

I wrote a couple of weeks ago about work getting cheaper rather than disappearing. This feels like the same story, just one step further along.

What I think IT services firms need to do

Selling AI the same way we sold people won't work. A rate card with fewer heads on it still loses.

The report is clear on what people are still needed for: checking the output, making decisions, getting buy-in, working out what's worth building. Agents need looking after too. One engineer read every transcript from his agent for two months, and he still checks it every couple of weeks.

So the shift is from selling hours on tasks to owning outcomes, sitting close to the client's real problems, and keeping these systems running as the models change. The firms that do that will be fine.

One caveat

It's 58 US companies, all Anthropic customers, picked for variety rather than as a proper sample. It's interviews, not hard numbers, and the authors say so themselves. Still, I think it's an early signal worth paying attention to.
