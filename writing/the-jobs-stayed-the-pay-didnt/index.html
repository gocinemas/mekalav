The jobs stayed. The pay didn't.

12 November 2026 · Vikram Mekala

Most of the talk about AI and jobs is about jobs disappearing. I think there's a quieter story that matters more.

The job stays. It just stops mattering who does it.

Economists Masao Fukui, Emi Nakamura and Jón Steinsson have a name for this: the commoditisation of labour. Their paper is about something older than AI, but it explains a lot about where AI might take us.

Automation and commoditisation aren't the same thing

Automation means a machine takes over the work, so there are fewer jobs.

Commoditisation is different. The work still needs people, but it gets simplified and standardised until almost anyone can do it. Firms stop being picky about who they hire, and people become easy to replace. There are plenty of jobs, but the pay is lower.

We've seen this before

McDonald's scripted almost every step in the kitchen. Warehouse staff follow instructions from a headset, and training dropped from weeks to days. Call centre staff read answers off a screen.

Nobody in those jobs was automated out. They just became interchangeable.

The pay followed. In the 1980s, the biggest US employers paid almost 60% more than small firms. By the 2010s it was about 20%.

I know this from the inside

At BP I ran a six-month transition handing people, processes and systems over to an incoming vendor. The whole job was making sure the work no longer depended on who was doing it.

AI does the same thing, only faster. Developers review generated code instead of writing it. Service desks pick from suggested answers. PMs get status reports drafted for them. More people can do the work, so firms get less picky about who they hire.

The documents are the easy part

Anyone who's run a transition knows the hard part is what never got written down. Things like:

  • knowing who needs a heads-up before a change goes live
  • knowing which "known issue" you can ignore and which one you can't
  • knowing why that old workaround is still there, and what breaks if you take it out

That know-how is what keeps a person hard to replace. It's also what gets lost when a role is standardised without much thought.

If you lead an enterprise IT team

  • Before you standardise a role with AI, ask what you'd lose if that person left tomorrow. Protect that.
  • Don't judge AI rollouts on cost per head alone. Track rework, incidents and decisions that had to be reversed.
  • Bring the people who know the unwritten stuff into the rollout early. Don't just ask them to test it at the end.

If you work in one

  • Stop asking "will AI replace me?" Ask which part of your work still needs you specifically, and do more of it.
  • Spend more time where decisions get made, not just where the work gets done.

If AI makes the doing easy, what's left to get paid for is judgment.

The paper: The Commoditization of Labor, Fukui, Nakamura and Steinsson (2026).
